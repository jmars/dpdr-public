# What the traditions knew — Source Library passages

Primary-source passages, found via [Source Library](https://sourcelibrary.org) (a library of
AI-translated historical texts), in which contemplative traditions describe the **collapse
mechanism** — clearly enough to show they understood the phenomenon, long before any mechanism
was known.

**Method.** Source Library's public API: `GET /api/search?q="<phrase>"` → book + page hits;
`GET /api/books/<id>` → metadata and page ids; `GET /api/pages/<page_id>` → the full page,
including the AI English translation. Quotation below is from those translations, with the
`<term>`/`<gloss>` markup and zero-width padding stripped and whitespace normalised. Section
headings and bracketed glosses in the source are kept where they carry meaning. A second pass was run
for Tibetan (§7).

## 1. Theravāda — the Ten Corruptions of Insight

The clearest statement in the whole sweep. The *Visuddhimagga* devotes a chapter of its
treatment of insight to the states that arise **when the practice is going well** — and names
the trap explicitly in the tenth item.

> At that stage, due to this embryonic insight, the 10 *vipassanupakkilesa* (corruptions of
> insight, things that make insight dull) arise to that yogi who has begun the practice of
> insight. In truth, the corruptions of insight do not arise to: 1. A noble disciple who has
> already attained the realization. 2. One who practices wrongly (beginning with a collapse of
> morality). 3. One who has abandoned the meditation practice. 4. An indolent person (even if
> they practiced correctly from the beginning). **They arise only to the child of good family
> who practices properly, applies effort repeatedly, and has begun the practice of insight.**
>
> But what are those 10 corruptions? … 1. *Obhasa* — Radiance/Light … 4. *Passaddhi* —
> Tranquility. 5. *Sukha* — Happiness. 6. *Adhimokkha* — Conviction/Faith. 7. *Paggaha* —
> Exertion/Effort. 8. *Upatthana* — Mindfulness/Presence. 9. *Upekkha* — Equanimity.
> **10. *Nikanti* — Attachment/Craving.**

**Source:** Buddhaghosa, *Visuddhimagga* (Path of Purification), ch. 20, "Description of the
Purification by Knowledge and Vision of What Is and Is Not the Path" — Thai edition, p. 1111.
https://sourcelibrary.org/book/visuddhimagga-path-of-purification-in-thai

**What it shows.** Two of the model's most counter-intuitive results, stated 1,500 years early.
**(1)** The failure arises to the person practising **correctly and intensively** — explicitly
*not* to the one practising wrongly, the one who quit, or the indolent. That is the model's
finding that exposure rises with earnest, competent practice. **(2)** The tenth corruption is
**nikanti — attachment/craving**: the corruption *is* clinging to the states themselves. That
is precisely the appraisal trap (mistaking the state for the goal). The chapter's own remedy
tradition — recognise and do not cling — is a recognition protocol, i.e. an *external* act, not
more effort.

## 2. Korean Sŏn — "wind-mad," and the warning that fighting it worsens it

A practice manual by Chinul warns of the stage where the danger appears, names the outcome,
and gives the counter-intuitive instruction: **do not fight the arising thoughts.**

> For three days and three nights, confront it head-on. You must not sit in a state of "doing
> nothing," nor should you be attached to "dead sitting" on the cushion. You must keep the
> practice alive. **If you fear that distracting thoughts are flying up in confusion, you
> absolutely must not fight with them. The more you fight them, the more intense they become.**
> Many people at this stage do not know whether to advance or retreat, and they cannot help but
> become **"wind-mad" — a term for mental breakdown or Zen sickness, ruining their entire
> lives.** You should go to the place where the distracting thoughts arise and gently lay them
> down. Turn your body around, get down on the floor, and walk…

**Source:** Chinul (1158–1210), *Susimgyŏl, chinsim chikkyŏl*, p. 86 (1700 edition).
https://sourcelibrary.org/book/susimgyol-chinsim-chikkyol-chinul

**What it shows.** Three things at once. **(a)** A named, catastrophic outcome of meditation —
"wind-mad," glossed in the text itself as *mental breakdown or Zen sickness, ruining their
entire lives*. **(b)** The failure arrives **"at this stage"** — i.e. at an advanced point, when
practice is established, not at the beginning. **(c)** The instruction is that **effort
intensifies it** ("the more you fight them, the more intense they become") — the model's result
that more force makes this worse, and the same inversion as the rescue post's "cheap and fixed
beats elaborate." The remedy given is **physical and outward** (get up, walk) — a redirect, not
more inward effort.

## 3. Chinese internal alchemy — "Fire Deviation"

A 1609 encyclopaedia carries a **woodcut illustration** of the failure — three figures in
agitation — with an explicit warning that includes *who* is at risk.

> …the text shifts to the private, internal risks of spiritual and physical cultivation,
> detailing the dangerous phenomenon of "Fire Deviation." … *Fire Deviation* (走火, *zǒuhuǒ*;
> literally "the fire escapes" or "the fire walks"): a term in traditional medicine and Daoist
> internal alchemy referring to the dangerous loss of control over one's internal energy (Qi)
> during meditation. … This woodcut illustration provides a vivid visual depiction of Fire
> Deviation, a condition where the "vital fire" of the body is misdirected… **If a practitioner
> lacks proper guidance or maintains an improper focus, this energy can "deviate" from its
> natural pathways, leading to symptoms that range from physical pain and illness to
> psychological obsession or madness.** This illustration serves as a stern cautionary artifact,
> warning readers that the path to spiritual health requires careful, disciplined practice to
> avoid these debilitating consequences.

**Source:** Wang Qi & Wang Siyi, *三才圖會* (*Sancai Tuhui*, Collected Illustrations of the
Three Realms), juan 74, p. 93 (1609).
https://sourcelibrary.org/book/sancai-tuhui-juan-74

**What it shows.** **(a)** A named, medically-catalogued meditation injury, depicted as a
*warning illustration* — the tradition built a cautionary artifact, not a promise. **(b)** The
named cause is **"lack of proper guidance"** — the model's holder requirement: recovery and
safety depend on an external structure, and its absence is what makes the deviation possible.
**(c)** "Improper focus" misdirecting internal energy is the inward-drive axis; the failure is
the drive *misdirected*, not insufficient.

## 4. Orthodox Hesychasm — *prelest*, and a chapter devoted to it

The Slavonic *Philokalia* — the Hesychast compendium — includes a dedicated chapter on the
danger, and the tradition has a single word for it.

> ### On silence, 7 chapters: … 7. **On delusion** (spiritual deception or *prelest*, where
> many other topics are also presented). leaf 108 on the reverse.

**Source:** *Добротолюбие* (Dobrotolyubie, 1793) — Slavonic *Philokalia*, Paisius Velichkovsky
(tr.), Nicodemus the Hagiorite & Macarius of Corinth (eds.), p. 10 (table of contents).
https://sourcelibrary.org/book/dobrotolyubie-1793-slavonic-philokalia-eds

**What it shows.** That a 7-chapter section of the central Hesychast compendium is **entirely
about the failure mode**, under a term (*prelest*, spiritual deception) that names exactly the
model's mechanism: a state arising from ascetic practice that presents as attainment and
deceives the practitioner. The existence of the category — with a dedicated chapter in a
prayer manual — is the evidence: *this is a known occupational hazard of the practice, and the
tradition documents it at the same canonical level as the method itself.*

> *Follow-up available:* the full chapter ("On delusion," leaf 108 reverse) was located in the
> table of contents but not read for this sweep — it is the highest-value next extraction, as
> it corresponds most directly to the mechanism (and to Ignatius Brianchaninov's later writings
> on the same term).

## 5. Syriac Christian asceticism — "he disguises himself as an angel of light"

A Syriac manuscript (Codex Vaticanus Syriacus 162) sets out **criteria for telling a true
spiritual state from a deceptive one** — because the deceptive one presents as light.

> Again, hear from me these things that I say to you, so that you may know the manifestation of
> the Holy Spirit, and how you may distinguish between truth and error. … **And these are the
> signs of the spirit of truth: first, humility, gentleness, and love toward God and toward men,
> and a pure and holy way of life, and patience in tribulations** … And when the Spirit speaks
> in a man, he does not speak for his own glory … And when he is persecuted and insulted, he
> does not grow angry and does not revile, but prays for his persecutors as our Lord did. **And
> when you see a man who says that "the Spirit is in me," and he exalts himself, and loves honor
> and titles, and has envy and zeal and contention, and his deeds are not like the Gospel of
> Christ, know that he is a false prophet, and the spirit of error speaks in him.** … Be
> therefore watchful and alert, that you may not be led astray by the guile of Satan, **for he
> disguises himself as an angel of light, to lead astray, if possible, even the elect.** The
> teaching of the Fathers on the discernment of spirits is concluded.

**Source:** *Syriac Theological Manuscript (Codex Vaticanus Syriacus 162)*, p. 41 (medieval).
https://sourcelibrary.org/book/syriac-theological-manuscript-codex-vaticanus-syriacus-162-anonymous

**What it shows.** **(a)** The named structure of the trap — *"he disguises himself as an angel
of light"* (2 Cor 11:14) — is the model's finding that the state **presents as the good**, which
is why it is not noticed. **(b)** The tradition supplies **explicit external criteria**: the test
is not how the state feels but **humility vs self-exaltation** ("does not speak for his own
glory" vs "exalts himself, and loves honor and titles"). That is the empty-leader post's rule
(follow where the self-content *terminates*) in ascetic form — and it is a *test*, which is what
a state that cannot read itself requires.

## 6. John of the Cross — obey the director *against your own certainty*

The sharpest statement of the **holder** requirement in the sweep. The soul in these states
holds its apprehension as certain — and is instructed to defer to an external person anyway.

> …a gift to distinguish between spirits: *discretio spirituum*, discernment of spirits.
> **Although the soul holds what she understands to be as certain and true as has been said,
> she still cannot neglect to follow what her spiritual teacher commands, even if it is very
> contrary to what she feels.** Thus, she may be judged and led in faith to the divine union,
> toward which she should walk more by believing than by understanding.

**Source:** Juan de la Cruz (John of the Cross), *Die geistliche Bücher und Schrifften* (German
translation of the *Ascent of Mount Carmel*), p. 324 (1697).
https://sourcelibrary.org/book/die-geistliche-bucher-und-schrifften-cruz

**What it shows.** This is **the model's second structural result, written as a rule**: the
state cannot read itself, so the check must come from *outside* — and the instruction is
explicit that the external authority **overrides the practitioner's own felt certainty**
("even if it is very contrary to what she feels"). That is precisely the "sensor must not live
in the failing subsystem" finding, and the holder requirement (post 1 §6, post 3 §5), stated as
a discipline. Note also *why* they can say it: the tradition assumes the state's felt certainty
is exactly what cannot be trusted.

## 7. Tibetan Mahāmudrā — "the signs of experience" vs "the signs of realization"

The second pass found it. Mahāmudrā's analysis of the four yogas treats the **signs of
progress** as having two species — and separates them *every time*.

> The third part, the signs, has two aspects: **the signs of experience and the signs of
> realization**. The sign of experience is the arising of an experience in the mind that is free
> from all complexity. **The sign of realization is the realization that all phenomena are, by
> their very nature, free from complexity.**

The same two-sign structure is then applied to each yoga in turn. For the yoga of One Taste:

> …the signs of experience and the signs of realization. The sign of experience is the arising
> of an experience where appearance and mind are non-dual. The sign of realization is the
> realization of all phenomena as having the nature of one taste.

**Source:** *Thor bu Phyag chen rnal 'byor bzhi bshad* (Explanation of the Four Yogas of
Mahāmudrā), Tshamdrak Monastery, Tibetan, p. 93.
https://sourcelibrary.org/book/thor-bu-phyag-chen-rnal-byor-bzhi-bshad-monastery

**What it shows.** This is the *nyams* / *rtogs pa* distinction — **experience** versus
**realization** — as a formal, repeated category in the path literature. The tradition is not
merely saying "be careful"; it is maintaining **two separate columns** for what the practitioner
may take to be the same event, because one is transient and one is not. That is the appraisal
mechanism named as a taxonomy: *the experience is not the realization*, and the two have
different signs.

Two further passages in the same text carry the anti-clinging instruction:

> …if that previous meditative concentration of one-pointedness is analyzed with the wisdom of
> Insight, **without being polluted by the stain of attachment**, it is seen as the very essence
> of non-dual wisdom. *(p. 75)*

> Small One-pointedness is **the mere experience** of the mind abiding. Medium One-pointedness
> is the attainment of stability in that experience. Great One-pointedness is when that very
> experience **mixes with the wisdom that realizes the essence**. *(p. 94)*

Note the gradation in that last one: the ladder **starts** at mere experience and only reaches
realization when the experience is *mixed with insight*. Experience alone is the bottom rung —
which is precisely the trap (mistaking the rung for the top).

**And the faults are catalogued as a formal category** in a separate Thadrak collection, which
specifies their removal:

> …the removal of faults in such meditation, which has two types: removing the faults of the body
> and removing the faults of the mind. The first: **if illnesses or harm from demons occur in the
> body**, they should be removed by relying on the application of channels and energies. The
> second: **if conceptual thoughts, disturbing emotions, sleepiness, or dullness occur in the
> mind**, they should be removed through mindfulness and awareness.

**Source:** *Thadrak Thor bu 014 gSung ngag rin po che'i lam 'bras bu dang bcas pa*, Thadrak
Temple, Tibetan, p. 238.
https://sourcelibrary.org/book/thadrak-thor-bu-014-gsung-ngag-rin-po-che-i-lam-bras-bu-temple

**What it shows.** The tradition has a **named remedy section** — "the removal of faults in
meditation" — subdivided into bodily faults (illness, "harm from demons") and mental faults
(conceptual thoughts, disturbing emotions, sleepiness, dullness). The failure mode is
*scheduled* into the curriculum, with prescribed corrections, rather than treated as a mark
against the practitioner.

**And the warnings are specific about which states are mistakes:**

> To say that one abandons the objects with new meditation and that old meditation is "better" is
> highly mistaken. **It is a mistake to hold onto "placing the mind in a state of comfort"** or
> "old meditation" and so on. That is not it.

**Source:** Khamtrul Ngawang Kunga Tenzin (1680–1728), *Introduction to the Preliminary Practice
of Mahāmudrā (Innate Union)*, p. 125 (1710).
https://sourcelibrary.org/book/introduction-to-the-preliminary-practice-of-mahamudra-1680-1728

**What it shows.** A named error, with the object of the error specified: **clinging to
comfortable states and calling them the practice.** That is the model's appraisal failure
(clinging to the state) and its "more is not deeper" inversion, in curricular form.

## 8. Summary, and what was not found

| tradition | native term | what it understood | mechanism feature |
|---|---|---|---|
| Theravāda (Buddhaghosa) | *vipassanupakkilesa*, *nikanti* | the states arise to those practising **correctly and hard**; the tenth corruption **is** clinging to them | failure scales with competent effort; the trap is the appraisal |
| Korean Sŏn (Chinul) | 風狂 "wind-mad" / Zen sickness | a named catastrophic outcome, at **"this stage"**; **fighting it makes it worse**; remedy is outward/physical | effort intensifies it; more force is wrong |
| Chinese alchemy (*Sancai Tuhui*) | 走火 "Fire Deviation" | a **catalogued injury**, warned against; caused by **lack of proper guidance** + improper focus | the holder requirement; the inward drive misdirected |
| Orthodox Hesychasm | *prelest* | a dedicated chapter on the failure mode in the central practice manual | the phenomenon is canonical, named, and expected |
| Syriac asceticism | "angel of light" | **the deceptive state presents as light**; external criteria (humility vs self-exaltation) to detect it | the state hides itself; needs an external test |
| **Tibetan Mahāmudrā** | *nyams* / *rtogs pa* — "signs of experience" vs "signs of realization" | **experience and realization kept as separate categories** with different signs, repeated per yoga; experience is the *bottom rung*; clinging to comfortable states is a named mistake; faults of meditation catalogued with remedies | the appraisal trap formalised as a taxonomy; the state is not the realisation |
| Catholic (John of the Cross) | *discretio spirituum* | **the soul's own certainty cannot be trusted**; obey the director against your feeling | the state cannot self-assess; the holder overrides it |

**The convergence, in one line.** Seven traditions, independently and in different vocabularies,
record the same five things the model measures: the danger scales with *correct* practice; the
state presents as attainment; effort makes it worse; it cannot be read from inside; and an
external person or test is required. None of them had the mechanism. All of them had the
*observations* — which is what the "the traditions encoded the control law" claim actually rests
on.

- **Tibetan** — *found on the second pass* (§7): the Four Yogas text separates "signs of
  experience" from "signs of realization" systematically, and the Thadrak collection carries a
  formal faults-of-meditation section. The first pass missed it because searching "nyams"
  returns Tibetan *nyams len* ("practice") rather than the warning literature; the working terms
  turned out to be **"signs"**, **"faults of meditation"**, and **"mistake"**.
- A **Sufi** passage on *istidraj* — searched, nothing usable in translation.
- The **full text of the Philokalia's "On delusion" chapter** (leaf 108 reverse) — located in
  the TOC, not yet read. Highest-value next extraction.
- **Zen *makyō*** as a term — the "wind-mad" finding covers the same ground, but *makyō* itself
  did not surface in this library's translations.
- Modern **Jungian / scholarly** framings of these concepts were not searched (out of scope for
  a primary-source sweep).
